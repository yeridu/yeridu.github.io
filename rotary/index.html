What we are trying to change

Seven modules, because our students named seven needs.

1Why Toledo
  • Toledo has the highest level of multidimensional poverty of any district in Belize. Nationally 35.7% of people lived in multidimensionally poor households in September 2021, and the rural rate runs at about double the urban rate
  • Toledo Community College is the only secondary school in Punta Gorda. It draws students from the town and from Maya and Garifuna villages across the district
  • So if young people here are to be reached at all, the school is where it happens

Statistical Institute of Belize, Multidimensional Poverty Index, September 2021

2What the published research finds
  • Across the English-speaking Caribbean, about 23.5% of adolescents show symptoms of a mental-health problem — between one in four and one in five — pooled from 28 studies and 65,034 young people
  • In Belize the suicide rate rose from 6.99 to 11.38 per 100,000 between 2019 and 2023, and attempts are most common at ages 15 to 19, the ages in Forms 1 to 4
  • A 2024 study in Toledo District reported three themes in how gender-based violence works here: what drives it; control, power and patriarchy; and how women respond

What actually makes prevention work

What the evidence says

Ten elements, and what each one made us do.

4 Design — essential
  1. A plan built on a clear theory of change, fitted to the local contextTHRIVE: designed from a survey of TCC's own students, with school climate as the mechanism
  2. Address several drivers of violence togetherTHRIVE: seven modules working as one system, not a single topic
  3. Support for survivorsTHRIVE: the NSPCC 4Rs, a suicide-risk response pathway, referrals agreed with local services in advance
  4. Work with men and women, boys and girlsTHRIVE: whole-class delivery, plus a module on masculinities and boys' health
2 Implementation — essential
  1. Enough intensity for people to reflect and practiseTHRIVE: 30 sessions of 40 minutes plus a booster, across the school year
  2. Enough well-selected, trained and supported staffTHRIVE: a trained facilitator carries the first year and hands over to teachers from the second
4 Where relevant to the approach
  1. Group activities that build empowerment and good relationshipsTHRIVE: every session is activity-based, not a lecture
  2. Participatory learning: critical reflection and communication skillsTHRIVE: debates, role-play and storytelling in each module
  3. A clear, user-friendly manual, followed systematicallyTHRIVE: a written facilitator manual covering all 30 sessions
  4. Age-appropriate design with an engaging pedagogy, for childrenTHRIVE: written for Forms 1 to 4, ages 12 to 17

One assembly changes nothing. Changing a norm takes repetition, practice and reinforcement over time.

The ten elements and their three groupings are from Jewkes R, Willan S, Heise L, et al. Int J Environ Res Public Health 2021;18:12129. The wording is simplified for a general audience; the line beneath each element is ours, not the paper's. The needs behind those lines are the seven findings on the earlier slide, not repeated here.

Why these areas, in Rotary's own words

Peacebuilding and conflict prevention covers "education for youth on constructive ways to prevent, manage, and transform conflict" and "facilitated dialogue, communication". Disease prevention and treatment covers "prevention and treatment services for physical and mental illnesses". Maternal and child health covers "education about and enhanced access to family planning and other sexual and reproductive health interventions" and "interventions to reduce the transmission and impact of sexually transmitted disease in adolescents".

Two limits we will not talk around

The same policy excludes "general health education" from disease prevention, and it funds adolescent sexual and reproductive health work only when it is carried out inside the health care system under licensed medical supervision. That is why Hillside Health Care is a partner in this study rather than an afterthought. And basic education and literacy is not on this list: it covers literacy, numeracy and teacher development, so the honest fit there is our teacher training, not the content of any module.

The part most programmes leave out

We wrote down, in advance, what would make us stop.

How one score is made
1
Everyone answers on the same 5-point scale.
1 Strongly disagree2 Disagree3 Not sure4 Agree5 Strongly agree
2
We average one person's answers within one section.
A real example — the four acceptability statements on the student survey
"I would be interested in lessons that help students handle stress and strong feelings."Agree · 4
"I would like lessons about healthy friendships and dating relationships."Strongly agree · 5
"I would like lessons that teach how to make safer choices about alcohol and drugs."Agree · 4
"I would like lessons about how to take care of our sexual and reproductive health."Not sure · 3
(4 + 5 + 4 + 3) ÷ 4 = 4.0 — this student's acceptability score
3
We average those person scores across the whole group.

The same three sections are scored the same way for students, parents, teachers, the principal, school staff and local informants: acceptability (do you want it?), appropriateness (does it fit here?) and feasibility (can this school actually run it?).

4
There is no single overall score.

This is the part people expect and we deliberately did not do. We never add the three sections together, because a high score in one cannot cancel a low score in another. Each section is read against the same threshold on its own, and the weakest of the three decides the colour. A curriculum that everyone wants, that fits Belize, but that the school cannot actually run is not a green light. It is an amber one, and the thing to fix is the running of it.

Green — proceed

3.5 or above on all three sections — the average answer sits between "Agree" and "Strongly agree". Plus at least 60% of teachers willing, and no fundamental safeguarding barrier.

Amber — proceed only with changes

3.0 to 3.5 — the average sits between "Not sure" and "Agree". We may proceed only if the concerns can genuinely be fixed by adapting the curriculum or strengthening the training.

Red — do not proceed

Below 3.0 — the average is at "Not sure" or worse. Also red: teacher willingness below 40%, substantial community opposition, or safeguarding capacity that is not adequate.

These thresholds were set before we collected a single response, and they are published. If Toledo says no, the answer is no.

TIME 14:00-16:30. This is the credibility slide, so do not rush the arithmetic. Walk the left column in order: one scale, one average per person, one average per group. Read the four student statements out loud -- they are the actual printed items, and hearing them tells the room exactly what we are asking their children. Then move right and translate each threshold back into words: green means the average person agreed; amber means the average person was somewhere between not sure and agreeing; red means the average person was not sure or worse. Then say the point of the whole slide: we published the conditions under which we would abandon our own project, before we had any data, so that we could not move the goalposts afterwards. "Substantial community opposition" is a stopping condition in writing, and this club is part of the community whose opposition would count. If you think this is wrong for Toledo, we would rather hear it at this table than find out after two years.

One module, from the inside

Masculinities and boys' health

Four sessions, in order: name it — what is inside the box we put boys in; feel it — what that rigidity costs them; connect it — how those norms lead to violence; act on it — what each student will do differently.

Video unavailable offline. The four sessions are described above — move on.

Why this module is in the curriculum: at TCC in May 2025, boys were about three times more likely than girls to agree with statements that blame the victim of violence (32.1% against 11.7%). The module is written for boys and girls together.

Where we could go from here

Four things to explore together — none needing a decision today.

  • 01 Take part, if you would like to. One of the five Phase 1 groups is key local informants — adults in Toledo who work with young people. It is one short survey, or one interview if you would rather talk than write. That is the whole of it.
  • 02 An Interact club at TCC — as an idea, not a proposal. Interact is Rotary's own programme for ages 12 to 18, which happens to be Forms 1 to 4. We are not putting it forward. We would like to work through with a few of you what it would really involve, and whether it suits the school at all.
  • 03 Compare notes with us. We have read the research on what makes these programmes work. You know Toledo — the school year against the harvest, the languages a form has to be printed in, how to reach parents. Together it gets better than either of us manages alone.
  • 04 Later, and only if Phase 1 says proceed. If the findings support going forward, we would welcome a conversation about whether a Rotary grant is a fit, given how the modules line up with the areas of focus. Not today.
